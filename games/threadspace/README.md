# ThreadSpace

A space game in three.js. You own a frigate, the **Kestrel**, and you can do everything on her yourself: walk her decks,
sit at her helm, climb into her turret, launch her one fighter (the **Wren**) from the bay, step out of her airlock in a
suit, and board a pirate corvette with a rifle. Two crew fly and shoot for you when you are not in the seat. Stations
have a mission board, a market, a shipyard and a bar. Three sectors are joined by **threads**, mapped wormholes with
one gate each.

The screen is split: the 3D view on the left, a panel on the right that changes with where you are (ship, nav, crew,
missions, fleet; a station's board, trade and yard; a mission's radio).

## How to play

From the title screen:

- **New captain**: the campaign. Dress your captain at the locker, walk to the helm, fly to Haven Station (J to dock),
  take a job, and go. One loop (bounty, trade, the Wren, a boarding, a space walk) takes 10 to 15 minutes. It saves to
  this browser when you dock or jump; **Continue** picks it up.
- **Drop-in mission**: about 15 minutes. Your ship, crew and credits are dealt to you and you arrive in the middle of a
  standoff: pirates hold a hauler and want a ransom before their thread opens. Pay, haggle on the radio, call the law,
  fight, or board her. Ends with a debrief and a grade. Each mission comes from a seed, so `?mission=5` is always the
  same one.
- **Shipyard**: change the Kestrel's hull, wings, drives, detail and the colours of every room. Save, go back to the
  game, and **Refit her now** on the Ship tab. Designs are kept in this browser; **Download** saves one as a file.

## Controls

| where | keys |
|---|---|
| anywhere | **Tab** pauses and opens the command map · **V** third person or your own eyes |
| walking | W A S D · mouse looks · **E** uses what you face |
| the helm / the Wren | mouse or arrows steer · A D roll · W S throttle · Space or click fires (Wren) · T targets · V chase view · **J** jumps a thread or docks · E stands up or lands |
| the turret | mouse aims · click or Space fires · T target · E climbs down |
| space walk | W S A D · R F up and down · Shift burns · E grabs a pod or goes back in |
| boarding | W A S D · mouse aims · click or Space fires · R reloads · hold E at the bridge console to take her |
| the command map | click a contact to give an order or a chain of orders · drag orbits · wheel zooms |

Click the view to take the mouse; Esc gives it back.

## The carrier (a prototype)

`carrier.html` (**The carrier** on the title screen) is a test bed for where ThreadSpace is heading: ships built like
real machines. She is a 288 m carrier laid out like a warship, her crew living in two drums that turn round a
weightless spine for their gravity. (The first carrier, 400 m with three decks and gravity only while her drive burns,
is archived in `archive/carrier-v1/`.)

- **Your first week aboard.** **Join as a new hand** and you come aboard as the most junior apprentice on the ship. Five officers stand at their posts and give you orders: report to the coxswain, read the watch bill, find your bunk and turn in until you are called for your watch, muster with your watch section, put out the damage control chief's drill fire and mend a drill electrical fault, do rounds of the repair lockers, score three hits on a target sled a tug tows past the dorsal turret, draw a vac suit, then hit a raider three times from the turret, fly a fighter home down the lane yourself, turn the ship 90° at the helm, and fly her to a waypoint 15 km off and stop on it. Each order you finish can promote you (apprentice, crewman, gunner, flight cadet, helmsman) and open up more of the ship: the turrets, the fighters, the helm, and raids that come on their own. A duty card shows your rank, your order and where to go, a gold chevron hangs over the officer you are looking for, and the cutaway marks the place in gold. Your progress is saved in the browser: **Carry on** wakes you in your bunk. **Free roam** opens the whole ship from the start.
- **Spin drums round a weightless spine.** Amidships, inside one armoured sleeve, two drums turn opposite ways at 3.7 turns a minute, so their rooms feel nearly 1 g. Each has two decks of forty rooms round a ring corridor: quarters, messes, the galley, the sick bay, the gym, workshops, the ship's office, and spares used as stores. To get into one, drop through a hatch in the spine's floor and catch a handhold on the hub ring turning past underneath. That puts you in a slow ring at a tenth of a g. Walk round it to a lift. A lift down a spoke takes nine seconds and you feel the weight come on as it goes. Coming back, the lift lets you out in the hub ring, and from anywhere in it you push off up into the spine. Ladders are climbed, not faded through. Walk the ring corridor and the floor rises ahead of you, all the way round. The rest of the ship is weightless: the bridge tower and engineering aft (grip shoes), the spine and the crew tunnel that you float along hand over hand, and the airless hangar, which you cross through its airlock and along a gangway to your fighter, clipped on to its safety wire.
- **A ship that is lived in.** Every kind of room has its own finish and light. The bridge and the combat information centre are dim, with padded walls and blue light. The messes and quarters have warm lamps. The galley has white tile and the sick bay clinical light. The workshops and engineering show bare structure under work lamps. The ship's frames stand up the walls, and cable trays and colour-banded pipes run along the deckheads. Scuffs and grime gather low down. The bridge, the CIC (with a turning plot of the battle space), the mess, the galley, the crew's quarters (three-high bunks with curtains and reading lamps), the sick bay and engineering control are each built as a set of their own. The spin-drum carrier's hangar is an airless workplace: deck crews in vac suits, crates under cargo nets, tool chests clamped to the deck with their tools on tethers, a catwalk up the starboard wall. You cross it weightless along a lattice gangway from the airlock, with a spur to each fighter ending at a frame of grab bars up to her cockpit: pull yourself along its rails or push off and drift down the middle, clipped by your lanyard to the orange safety wire overhead, so you can never float off into the bay. In there the sound is your own suit: its fans and your breathing in the helmet. Posters and notices hang on the walls, among them a mess menu, the ship's crest and a "days without a hull breach" board. Under everything you hear the ship: the air handlers' hiss, the electrical hum, the rumble of the drive through the frames, louder in engineering and the hangar and quietest in your bunk, and now and then the hull creaking. `?post=0` turns off the bloom aboard for a slow computer.
- **No wasted space.** As on a real warship, the hull is packed. Behind the walls of the fixed hull, sealed spaces hold the reactor, the drive, heat sinks, propellant tanks, magazines and ordnance rooms, the barbettes and hoists of ten turrets, fighter fuel and spares, air trunking, bilge tanks and the sensor array. About 75% of the fixed hull is accounted for, and the drums are all rooms.
- **Finding your way.** Coloured lines on the passage floors lead to the bridge, the hangar tunnel, the hangar airlock, engineering and the drums (to the spine and its lifts). Arrows show the way, and each line turns off through its own door. Hanging signs list what is ahead in the same colours. "You are here" deck plans hang at every ladder. Navy-style frame plates (FR 120 is 120 m aft of the bow) and the top of the screen say exactly where you are.
- **Real flight.** She flies on thrust alone: three main drives and eight clusters of manoeuvring thrusters, each pushing from where it sits. A 180° turn takes her about a minute.
- **Machinery you can see.** Engineering control looks through its window into the reactor: the plasma ring burning inside its field coils, lit by its own glow and the work lamps, dimming to an ember on emergency power. Life support control looks into the atmosphere plant.
- **Raids and real damage.** Every few minutes a raider, a red corvette you can see, comes in from one side and makes firing passes down it. The speakers call action stations and every pressure door aboard shuts. Each of its shells flies from its guns; where one meets the hull it goes in on a straight line: it holes the first compartment it reaches to space, punches through into the ones behind, and wrecks what it passes. Most hits land in the outer spaces (tanks, berthing, machinery), as a warship's armoured core is meant to make them; some get through to where you are.
- **No shields: armour, point defence and torpedoes.** Nothing aboard is anything you could not build. An armour belt stops some of the raider's shells outright and blunts the rest. The raider fires torpedoes, homing missiles that burn hard and weave in their last stretch; eight automatic point defence guns on sponsons round the hull (three down each side, one on top, one underneath) pick them off, filling the sky with tracer, and kill about three in four. The ship fires her own torpedoes at the raider, from six tubes in her bow and from the broadside tubes in the hangar walls, and the raider's point defence shoots at those. Ten heavy turrets, each on its barbette over a magazine, fire back too: a dorsal and a ventral pair at the bow, three pairs along the hangar and a pair at the stern. Their crews fire them, not very well. Man one yourself from its hatch (the bow dorsal turret's is up its hoist trunk from the upper deck, forward of the drums; the hangar turrets' are off the crew tunnel above the hangar and the keel passage below it): the sight follows the mouse and the heavy turret follows the sight at its own pace, the circle showing where the guns point and the diamond where to aim to hit the raider as it moves; diamonds mark the torpedoes. For practice there is a gunnery drill: a yellow tug streams an orange target sled down the ship's side on straight, steady passes, nothing shoots back, and the sight counts your hits. The hoist keeps the ready rack filled from the magazine. Hits on the turret's barbette slow it and then jam it, a wrecked magazine stops the hoist, and the firing cutout will not let you shoot through your own ship.
- **A broadside you aim with the ship.** Sixteen torpedo tubes in each hangar wall launch cold, straight out sideways, and each torpedo lights its motor clear of the hull. At the helm the sight shows each side's broadside arc and the raider's bearing: turn her so a side bears, with the armoured sleeve toward the raider, and click to fire a salvo of four from that side. A side reloads in 30 seconds and is fed from its own two ordnance rooms, so a wrecked magazine silences it. With nobody at the helm, the combat information centre fires them. Two railguns fire straight out of the hangar walls too, one a side, a two-tonne slug at 9 km/s: they cannot train, so you turn and roll her until the raider will cross that side's line (the plot says how far off it is and which way to roll) and press **Q**. Every shot pushes the ship the other way, a railgun's enough to see on the instruments; full flight assist takes the drift out again.
- **The open drum section.** The armoured sleeve round the two drums is an open tube, as the ship designer draws it. From the bridge's forward windows, from outside and from the helm's outside view, you see in past its ends: the drums' end walls turning, the spokes' lift tubes, the hub rings and the spine between the bow and the hangar, held by heavy struts. That structure is armoured but exposed: a shell that comes in at an end can hole the spine or a spoke.
- **Duty suits.** Outside the drums everyone wears a fitted pressure suit as working uniform, its hood and visor rolled down behind the neck; in the drums the crew wear the off-duty uniform, a jacket in their department's colour; in the airless hangar the deck crews wear heavy suits. You come aboard in your duty suit. The lockers beside each drum's lift doors are where you change (E), and the lift won't take you up to the hub without it; when you turn in at your bunk you are out of it, so the first thing after you are called is the lockers by the lift. The grey SUIT AIR lockers in the passages give you fresh bottles.
- **Condition one.** When action stations is called, the ship sets condition one, and ten seconds later she pumps the air out of every crew space, so a hit cannot blow anyone out with it. On the spin-drum carrier only the fixed hull is pumped down: the drums keep their air. Your visor shuts by itself on thin air (you see its edge, and your suit's air counts down at the top left), and opens again when the ship is repressurised after the fight. Caught without your suit, you black out, and a damage control party suits you up where you lie.
- **Heat and reaction mass.** Everything aboard ends up as heat, and in space the only way to be rid of it is to radiate it. The long radiators aft glow from dark red to orange as the heat sinks fill. They are fragile, so at action stations she folds them away and the sinks start to fill: a fight is on a clock. Fill them and the point defence holds its fire and the main drive is held back until she cools. **G** at the helm spreads or folds them by hand. The main drive burns real reaction mass, and the helm shows how much velocity change is left (the rocket equation). A hole in a tank leaks it. The cutaway's status board shows the heat, the radiators and the propellant with everything else.
- **Flip and burn.** There are no brakes in space. **N** at the helm lays in a waypoint 15 km off; the plot shows the range, how fast you are closing, and how far it would take to stop, counting the minute she takes to turn end for end, during which she coasts. When that reaches the range it calls FLIP AND BURN: put her nose on the crossed circle (retrograde) and burn the other way. It counts the three seconds the drive takes to light from cold.
- **Feeling the drive.** The main drive pushes her at 0.14 g, and you feel it aboard. From cold, the ship calls "Stand by for main drive" and the helm counts down three seconds before she lights. In a drum, down leans about 8° toward the stern, and walking toward the bow is a little harder. In grip shoes in the fixed hull you brace, and walk slower toward the bow. Floating in the spine or the crew tunnel, the push carries you aft until you hold on (**W**). At the helm she presses you back into the seat. Walking round a drum you feel the Coriolis effect too: heavier going spinward, lighter going the other way.
- **The fighter's guns.** Out in a fighter, hold the mouse button to fire her twin cannon. The head-up display brackets the raider (or the target sled) with its range, hull and closing speed, and an amber pip shows where to put her nose so the rounds meet it. One round does a fraction of what a turret's shell does, but there are nine a second, from a drum of 600 that is refilled on the pad. The raider's turrets fire back once you are within about 1.3 km. Each shot is aimed where you will be if you fly on as you are, so keep changing your velocity: a fighter that sits still is shot dead in under half a minute, one that keeps sliding is rarely hit. Your hull shows on the head-up display once it is hit. At nothing you are dead in space, and flight control takes the fighter and brings you in; she is mended and rearmed on her pad.
- **Heat-seekers and flares.** Your fighter carries four heat-seeking missiles. Point her nose at something hot (or pick a contact with X and get it near the nose): the head-up display circles what the seeker has locked and a tone rises, and Q fires. A seeker goes for whatever is brightest, friend or foe, and the carrier with her radiators out is the brightest thing in the sky. The escort fighters carry them too. When one is tracking you, MISSILE flashes with its range and a tone beeps: let go of the drive (a burning drive is brighter than a flare) and drop flares with G. Every ship flares against missiles, so yours do not always hit, and the raider's point defence shoots at them.
- **Air.** Every compartment holds air. A hole to space vents it, quickly for a small room and slowly for a big one. Inside you see the hole, the hot torn edge, and the dust streaming out through it, and the wind pulls you toward it. A hole in a drum turns with the drum, and the repair drones follow it round. The room's lamps go red, and the top left shows the pressure. Below 30 kPa your sight closes in, and after a few seconds you black out; a damage control party drags you to the nearest room with air. Pressure doors close round a leak by themselves. Open one by hand with **E** (never onto a vacuum), and at action stations it shuts again behind you.
- **Repairs.** Four repair drones fly out of the drone bay to seal each hole; with a patch kit you can plug one yourself first (hold **E**). Life support then puts the air back. The reactor, the main drive, every manoeuvring cluster, life support and the drones each run on the health of their compartments, so the main drive weakens as it is hit, and with the reactor wrecked she is on emergency power: dim red lights and no main drive. The cutaway's status board shows all of it, with venting spaces flashing red and airless ones blue.
- **Damage control.** A hit can start a fire, an electrical fault or a coolant leak in the room it hurt. A klaxon or chimes sound, the speakers call it out with its place ("Fire, fire, fire. Fire in the galley. Forward drum, outer deck, frame 91, segment 17."), and a job card shows what to do. Fetch the kit: extinguishers from the red cabinets and patch kits from the yellow lockers in the passages, tool kits from the workshops. For an electrical fault, isolate the supply at the switchboard first. Then find the place by its frame number and hold **E**. Leave a fire and it spreads; leave a fault and it starts a fire; leave a leak and the section is lost. A fire in a room that vents goes out. The cutaway marks the job in red. You can call a drill from the damage control centre. `?dc=0` turns the raids off, and `?voice=0` keeps the alarms without the spoken calls (a call with a place in it uses a voice installed on your computer; nothing goes over the network).
- **Voices.** The officers, your leading hand and the ship's broadcast speak in recorded voices, each their own, from all over Earth: an Irish coxswain, a Nigerian damage control chief, a South African gunnery officer, an Australian pilot, a Texan commander on the bridge, a Glaswegian leading hand who wakes you for your watch, and the ship's broadcast through the tannoy. Each voice was designed from a short description and the lines recorded once with Google's Gemini text-to-speech and ship as ordinary audio files (`assets/voice/`), so the game calls no AI service. A line with a place in it (a fire's frame number) is spoken by your computer's own voice instead. `tools/voices.mjs` records new lines; see `CLAUDE.md`.
- **The crew.** Fourteen realistic people sit, work and walk at their posts, and off-watch hands go about the drums, in department colours: blue-grey for command, sand for flight, rust for engineering.
- **Fighters you can launch.** Climb into one of the twelve fighters in the hangar. Her drive spins up, flight control opens the bay door on her side and the launch lane runs you out into space. She flies by Newton and nothing else: no drag, no top speed. Hold W and the main drive burns, harder the longer you hold it; let go and it cuts, and you keep the speed. Add Shift for a hard burn, past the drive's rating, at over 8 g: lying in the couch you can take it for a while on the juice it pumps into you, then the view greys from the edges and you black out, the drive cuts, and you come round a few seconds later. Small jets in four blocks turn her with a pop to start the turn, a coast and a pop to stop it. To stop, flip onto the crossed circle and burn. The tanks are finite: the drive's fuel and the jets' cold gas run down as you fly, and the deck crew top them up on the pad when you land.
- **A cockpit you sit in.** The fighters are modelled in Blender, outside and in, as a craft built for space, not for air: a pressure capsule for the pilot, propellant tanks slung alongside, a gimballed drive bell, radiator panels instead of wings, and a thruster quad on an arm at every cluster the physics fires. The drive's flame pours out gas that spreads and cools from white through blue to violet and is gone in a few seconds; each jet pop glints and flings a brief burst of frosty gas. Every sound she makes is worked out live from what she is doing: the drive's deep throb and roar, each valve's pop and hiss, the hull groaning under load, the cockpit settling and ticking around you at rest. There are no recordings. There are also side numbers, landing gear that folds away in flight, navigation lights and a strobe. Inside is a working cockpit: a padded tub, a labelled caution strip, gauges and a gear handle, side consoles of switches, guarded switches and knobs under their placards, a circuit-breaker panel, a canopy jettison handle, ejection handles, a throttle quadrant, a stick between your knees, your own legs and a kneeboard, and three live screens in bezels ringed with buttons: her thrusters firing, a plot of the carrier and the lane, and the approach. A head-up display shows the nose, a pitch ladder and course against the carrier's deck, speed, the fuel and jet gas left, range to the bay, where you are going, and the aim.
- **Landing yourself.** A lane of lit rings runs straight out from the bay. Fly to its far ring, turn in, and come down it slowing as you go. Each ring asks for a speed, and the next one turns green when you are right, amber when you are fast or off the line, red when you are much too fast. Go through the last ring slowly and flight control catches you, sets you on your pad, and grades the landing. Near the bay, **E** still hands you to flight control instead. The carrier is solid: brush her slowly and you scrape along her plating in sparks; hit her harder and your hull takes it (about 26 m/s, measured against the part of her you hit, is the end of you), and above 30 m/s you hole the compartment you hit as well. A turning carrier's bow can swing into a fighter sitting still. You can fly in through an open bay door (mind the parked fighters), but a shut one is a wall.
- **A ship's day.** A clock runs aboard (a four-hour watch is eight minutes), the ship's bell strikes every half hour, and three watch sections, red, white and blue, keep the seven watches in turn (the two short dog watches move the rota round by one each day). The watch bill on its board outside the ship's office shows who keeps which, the posts manned every watch, and your own line: Blue watch, damage control party. The crew follow it: the faces at the bridge, CIC, engineering and damage control change at the change of watch, the day workers are at their benches from 0800 to 1700, and in the drums off-watch hands eat in the messes at meal times, work out in the gym, sit at the workshop benches and walk the ring corridors. Before the change of watch they queue at the spoke lifts, and one or two ride the car with you. At action stations the drums empty up the spokes, and the duty card tells you where to close up by your rank: be late and your name goes in the book. Turn in at your bunk and your leading hand shakes you thirty minutes before your watch. Your section falls in at the amidships repair locker for its muster, and the roll is called at the bell: be there, or be adrift, with your name in the book. From 2200 to 0600 the passages go to red lighting.

The first week is the only story here so far. After it, the game is fighting off the raids: no missions yet.

The ship keeps flying whatever you do: leave the helm with her turning and she goes on turning (unless flight assist is on).

In a fighter, the attitude hold has two settings:

- **Hold** (the default): the mouse moves the aim, an amber diamond. She fires one pop of the jets to start turning towards it, coasts, and fires one pop sized to stop on it. Turning never changes where you are going: only a burn does.
- **Off**: the jets fire only while you push, and nothing stops a spin but you.

Nothing ever kills your drift for you. To stop, put the nose on the crossed circle and burn for about as long as you burned to get here. The jets are small: they nudge you, they cannot undo a burn. If their tank runs dry the HUD says **RCS DRY** and she will not turn until you are back on the pad.

On the HUD (and on the markers from the outside view):

- a circle with wings shows the way you are going relative to the carrier (prograde);
- a crossed amber circle shows the opposite way (retrograde: point the nose there and burn to stop);
- an amber diamond shows the aim, where the attitude hold is taking the nose;
- a diamond shows where to fly: the lane's far ring, then down the lane to the bay. Off screen, it becomes an arrow at the edge.

## State

Playable end to end on desktop with a keyboard and mouse. Not built: landing on planets, music, more than one drop-in
mission template, touch controls. Balance numbers are first guesses.

The drop-in mission's other captains talk from a script. If you run a local model with [Ollama](https://ollama.com),
they can answer in their own words instead: `node tools/llm-server.mjs`, then open `http://127.0.0.1:8787/?llm=on`.
It is off by default, and the game is complete without it.

## Run it locally

ES modules need a web server (opening `index.html` as a file will not work):

```bash
npx serve .            # or: python3 -m http.server 8000
```

Then open the address it prints. No install, no build.

## Make it yours

```bash
npx tiged The-Low-Code-Foundation/nodegx-gaming/games/threadspace my-game
```

`CLAUDE.md` is the map for an AI coder: where every system lives and how to add a sector, a mission, a crew member or a
ship.
